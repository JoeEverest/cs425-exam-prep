Find the Analysis Classes

≈ 20 minutes · pen and paper required at the end · cheat sheet · ← Lesson 2

Why this lesson. A use-case realization shows which objects collaborate to perform the use case. The classes you find here become the lifelines of Lesson 4's sequence diagram. Wrong classes here means a wrong diagram there.

What the exam asks

  • Develop a use-case realization: how objects collaborate to perform the use case.
  • Identify the participating boundary, control, and entity classes, and explain their responsibilities.
  • Assign behavior with responsibility-driven design.
  • Refine analysis classes into design classes without losing behavior.

The three stereotypes

StereotypeJobAsk yourselfHow many?
«boundary»Talks to the actor. Translates clicks into requests and results into displays. No business logic."Who faces the actor?"One per actor–use-case pair.
«control»Coordinates the flow of the use case. Runs the steps, applies the business rules, decides the branches."Who coordinates?"Usually one per use case.
«entity»Holds domain data and the rules that belong to that data. Lives longer than the use case."Who has the data?"One per domain concept in the flows.

On paper, draw each class either as a box with the stereotype in guillemets, or as the RUP symbol: boundary = a circle with a vertical bar on its left, control = a circle with an arrowhead on top, entity = a circle resting on a horizontal line. Both notations score; pick one and stay with it.

Memory trick: the boundary bar is a wall to the outside, the control arrow spins the loop, the entity circle sits on the ground — it persists.

From specification to classes in five moves

Input: the Enroll in Course specification from Lesson 2. Keep it beside you.

1

One boundary per actor–use-case pair

The Student actor uses Enroll in Course through one interface. Name it after the interaction: EnrollmentForm. Its only responsibilities: accept the request, show the result.

2

One control per use case

Name it after the use case: EnrollmentController. It owns the flow: verify prerequisites, check seats, record the enrollment, decide waitlist or rejection. The controller knows the order; it does not own the data.

3

One entity per domain concept in the flows

Underline the nouns in your main flow: student, course, enrollment. Each noun that carries data becomes an entity: Student, Course, Enrollment. The catalog and the waitlist can stay attributes of Course at this level.

The kiosk, the screen, and the button are not entities. They are the boundary's problem.

4

Assign responsibilities, step by step

Walk the specification. For each step ask the three questions from the table above. Write the result as a responsibility table — this is responsibility-driven design, and it is worth marks on its own.

Refine to design classes — without losing behavior

In design, each analysis class maps to something buildable. The professor's rule: every responsibility from move 4 must still have an owner after the refinement.

Analysis classDesign class(es)What changes
EnrollmentForm «boundary»EnrollmentForm (UI class)Gains concrete widgets and events; still no business logic.
EnrollmentController «control»EnrollmentController / EnrollmentServiceSame coordination role; now calls repositories instead of touching entities directly for persistence.
Student, Course, Enrollment «entity»Entity classes + CourseRepository, EnrollmentRepository «DAO»Data rules stay on the entities; loading and saving move to DAOs/repositories.

Checking BR-1 was a controller + entity responsibility in analysis. It still is in design — the repositories only fetch the data. If a check silently disappears during refinement, the consistency check fails.

Red-pen list: how students lose marks here

  • Boundary wired to entities. The form never touches Course or Enrollment. Everything goes through the control.
  • Business logic in the boundary. "The form verifies prerequisites" costs marks twice — here and again in Lesson 4.
  • A controller army. One use case, one control class. Ten controllers signal guessing.
  • Screen furniture as entities. Button, Screen, and Kiosk are not domain concepts.
  • The god controller. If the controller also stores capacity and prerequisites, the entities are empty shells. Data rules belong on the data.
  • Lost behavior in refinement. Every analysis responsibility must reappear in a design class.

Quick check

1 · Who is responsible for the BR-1 prerequisite check?

Rule checks are coordinated by the control, using data owned by entities. The boundary only carries the request and the answer.

2 · How many control classes does "Enroll in Course" need?

The control owns the whole flow of one use case, including its alternative and exceptional branches.

3 · Which link is wrong in a use-case realization?

A boundary never links directly to an entity. The control sits between them — that is the point of the pattern.

4 · "Course knows its capacity and its current enrollment count." Why does this belong on the entity, not the controller?

Responsibility-driven design: "who has the data?" answers "who checks the rule about that data". A controller that hoards data creates empty entities.

5 · During refinement, CourseRepository appears. What happened to the entity Course?

Refinement redistributes persistence work, never domain behavior. Course still knows its prerequisites and capacity; the DAO only fetches and stores it.

Grade yourself

  • Exactly one boundary and one control, named after the interaction and the use case.
  • Three entities: Member, Book, Loan — no Kiosk, Screen, or Scanner entity.
  • Every specification step has an owner in your responsibility list.
  • Boundary links only to the control; control links to the entities.
  • Refinement adds repositories without deleting any rule check.
